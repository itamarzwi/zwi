## Separable verbs (trennbare Verben)

Some German verbs are made of two parts: a short prefix (Präfix) and a base verb (Grundverb). **aufwachen** (to wake up) is auf + wachen, **anrufen** (to call) is an + rufen.

In the infinitive they are one word, but in a sentence they separate (hence the name!!). The verb part is conjugated and goes in second position like a normal verb, and the prefix goes to the *very end*:

<ExampleList>
  <Example de="Ich verb(fange) jetzt verb(an)." en="I'm starting now." note="anfangen" />
  <Example de="Wir verb(wachen) spät verb(auf)." en="We wake up late." note="aufwachen" />
  <Example de="Jess verb(steigt) in den Zug verb(ein)." en="Jess is getting on the train." note="einsteigen" />
</ExampleList>

These are separable verbs (trennbare Verben).

Like in the Perfekt, separable verbs create a **sentence bracket**. Two parts wrap around the sentence, and everything else goes between them.

<ExampleList>
  <Example de="Ich verb(rufe) verb(an)." en="I'm calling." />
  <Example de="Ich verb(rufe) Anna verb(an)." en="I'm calling Anna." />
  <Example de="Ich verb(rufe) Anna heute verb(an)." en="I'm calling Anna today." />
  <Example de="Ich verb(rufe) Anna morgen nach dem Mittagessen und vor dem Spiel verb(an)." en="I will call Anna tomorrow after lunch and before the game." />
</ExampleList>

<Callout title="Präsens for future tense">
The last example is in the Präsens in German, but in the future tense in English. In German we usually use the Präsens to talk about the future, as long as it's clear from context. A time word like heute or morgen is often enough: "Ich rufe dich morgen an" means "I'll call you tomorrow".
</Callout>

## Common separable verbs

| Infinitive  | er/sie/es   | English                |
| ----------- | ----------- | ---------------------- |
| aufstehen   | steht auf   | to get up              |
| aufwachen   | wacht auf   | to wake up             |
| einsteigen  | steigt ein  | to get on (e.g. the train)      |
| anrufen     | ruft an     | to call (on the phone) |
| anfangen    | fängt an    | to start               |
| einschlafen | schläft ein | to fall asleep         |
| mitkommen   | kommt mit   | to come along          |
| ankommen    | kommt an    | to arrive              |

<ExampleList>
  <Example de="Ich verb(rufe) dich morgen verb(an)." en="I'll call you tomorrow." />
  <Example de="Der Film verb(fängt) jetzt verb(an)." en="The film is starting now." />
  <Example de="Das Kind verb(schläft) schnell verb(ein)." en="The child falls asleep quickly." />
</ExampleList>

The verb part is conjugated exactly like the verb on its own. fangen is irregular (du fängst), so anfangen is too: du fängst an. The same goes for schlafen → er schläft ein.

Sometimes the meaning is easy to guess from the parts: kommen is to come, and mitkommen is to come along (mit means "with"). Sometimes it isn't: fangen is to catch, but anfangen is to start.

## Questions and negation

### Questions

Questions work the same as always. In a yes/no question, the verb moves to the front, and the prefix stays at the end:

<ExampleList>
  <Example de="verb(Wachst) du früh verb(auf)?" en="Do you wake up early?" />
  <Example de="verb(Kommst) du verb(mit)?" en="Are you coming along?" />
</ExampleList>

In a W-question, the question word comes first, then the verb, and the prefix still comes at the end:

<ExampleList>
  <Example de="Wann verb(wachst) du normalerweise verb(auf)?" en="When do you normally wake up?" />
  <Example de="Wen verb(rufen) sie verb(an)?" en="Whom are they calling?" />
</ExampleList>

<Callout title="Wen">
  Remember that "wer" changes depending on its case! "anrufen" takes an Akkusativ object, so when asking about that object, we use "wer" in the Akkusativ case - "wen".

  "**Wen** rufen sie an?" (Whom are they calling?)<br/>
  "Sie rufen **ihn** an" (They are calling him)
</Callout>

### Negation

When negating a full sentence, nicht usually goes at the very end. But with a separable verb, the prefix goes at the very end! What do we do?? 😨  
Just like in the Perfekt, the bracket always wins and the prefix claims the spot at the very end. So nicht goes right before the prefix, as close to the end as possible.

<ExampleList>
  <Example de="Ich rufe ihn adverb(nicht) an." en="I'm not calling him." />
  <Example de="Anna kommt adverb(nicht) mit." en="Anna isn't coming along." />
</ExampleList>

## Separable or not?

We've already seen some verbs with inseparable prefixes, like be-, ver- and er-. As the name says, those never split:

<ExampleList>
  <Example de="Ich verb(besuche) Anna." en="I'm visiting Anna." />
  <Example de="Ich verb(verstehe) das nicht." en="I don't understand that." />
</ExampleList>

So how can we tell them apart? Separable prefixes are mostly small words that also exist on their own, like ab, an, auf, aus, ein¹ and mit. Inseparable prefixes (be-, ver-, er-, ent-, ge-) are never words on their own.

This also affects word pronunciation! Separable prefixes are stressed (AUFstehen, ANrufen), inseparable ones never are (verSTEHen, beSUCHen).

Both aufstehen and verstehen are built on top of stehen, but only aufstehen is separable:

<ExampleList>
  <Example de="Ich verb(stehe) früh verb(auf)." en="I get up early." />
  <Example de="Ich verb(verstehe) dich." en="I understand you." />
</ExampleList>

<Footnote>1. This ein is not the article ein (a/an). It comes from in (in, into), like in einsteigen and einschlafen.</Footnote>
