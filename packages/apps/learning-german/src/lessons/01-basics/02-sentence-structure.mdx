## Subject, verb, object

The simplest German sentence is **subject - verb - object**, just like English. Some sentences need only a subject and a verb.

<Example de="Ich lerne Deutsch." en="I am learning German." note="subject - verb - object" />
<Example de="Anna schläft." en="Anna is sleeping." note="subject - verb" />

## Adjectives

Adjectives describe nouns: *big*, *small*, *new*, *old*. In German, an adjective in front of a noun gets an **ending**, and the ending depends on the noun's gender and the article before it. Changing a word's ending like this is called **declension**.

For now we only look at adjectives that describe the subject of the sentence (the one doing the action). The other situations will come later.

### der / die / das

After der, die or das, the ending is **-e**, except in the plural, where it is **-en**.

<GenderedTable
  rows={[
    ["der groß**e** Hund", "das groß**e** Haus", "die groß**e** Stadt", "die groß**en** Hunde"],
  ]}
/>

<ExampleList>
  <Example de="Der **große** Hund schläft." en="The **big** dog is sleeping." />
  <Example de="Die **kleine** Stadt ist schön." en="The **small** city is beautiful." />
  <Example de="Das **neue** Buch ist gut." en="The **new** book is good." />
</ExampleList>

<Callout title="The article shows the gender">
With der, die and das there is no mistaking the gender of the noun, so the adjective doesn't have to show it and simply gets -e.
</Callout>

### With ein / eine

After ein or eine, the ending depends on the gender. There is no ein in the plural, so the table uses meine (my):

<GenderedTable
  rows={[
    ["ein groß**er** Hund", "ein groß**es** Haus", "eine groß**e** Stadt", "meine groß**en** Hunde"],
  ]}
/>

<ExampleList>
  <Example de="Ein **großer** Hund schläft." en="A **big** dog is sleeping." />
  <Example de="Eine **kleine** Stadt ist schön." en="A **small** city is beautiful." />
  <Example de="Ein **neues** Buch ist gut." en="A **new** book is good." />
</ExampleList>

<Callout title="Why is ein different?">
ein has no ending of its own for masculine and neuter nouns, so we can't tell the gender from the article. The adjective shows it instead: -er (like der) and -es (like das).
</Callout>

### Without an article

Sometimes there is no article at all, for example with uncountable nouns (e.g. coffee, water, milk) or plural nouns. Then the adjective takes the same ending that the article would have had:

<GenderedTable
  rows={[
    ["kalt**er** Kaffee", "kalt**es** Wasser", "frisch**e** Milch", "klein**e** Hunde"],
  ]}
/>

<ExampleList>
  <Example de="Kalter Kaffee ist lecker." en="Cold coffee is delicious." />
  <Example de="Frische Milch ist teuer." en="Fresh milk is expensive." />
  <Example de="Kleine Hunde sind laut." en="Small dogs are loud." />
</ExampleList>

<Callout>
When there is no article at all, the adjective is the only word that can hint at the noun's gender: -er like der, -es like das, -e like die.
</Callout>

### After sein

When the adjective comes after a form of sein (is, are, am), it gets no ending - whatever the gender.

<ExampleList>
  <Example de="Der Hund ist **groß**." en="The dog is **big**." />
  <Example de="Die Stadt ist **klein**." en="The city is **small**." />
  <Example de="Das Buch ist **neu**." en="The book is **new**." />
</ExampleList>

## Time and place

Besides who does what, a sentence often says **when** and **where** something happens. In the middle of a sentence these go right after the verb.

<Callout title="A note on wording">
Time and place serve the same grammatical role in a sentence, so in these lessons "time specification" (or just "time") might also refer to a place, like "zu Hause" (at home).
</Callout>

### When?

A time word answers "when?". It is usually a single word.

| German | English |
| ------ | ------- |
| heute | today |
| morgen | tomorrow |
| jetzt | now |
| immer | always |
| oft | often |

<ExampleList>
  <Example de="Ich lerne **heute** Deutsch." en="I am learning German **today**." />
  <Example de="Anna schläft **jetzt**." en="Anna is sleeping **now**." />
  <Example de="Wir arbeiten **morgen**." en="We are working **tomorrow**." />
</ExampleList>

### Where?

A place answers "where?". It can be a single word like hier (here), or a short phrase like "in Marburg" or "zu Hause" (at home).

<ExampleList>
  <Example de="Wir wohnen **hier**." en="We live **here**." />
  <Example de="Ich arbeite **in Marburg**." en="I work **in Marburg**." />
  <Example de="Anna ist **zu Hause**." en="Anna is **at home**." />
</ExampleList>

### Together

When a sentence has both, time comes before place:

<ExampleList>
  <Example de="Ich arbeite green(heute) blue(in Marburg)." en="I am working blue(in Marburg) green(today)." />
  <Example de="Wir lernen green(jetzt) blue(zu Hause)." en="We are learning blue(at home) green(now)." />
  <Example de="Anna wohnt green(jetzt) blue(in Deutschland)." en="Anna lives blue(in Germany) green(now)." />
</ExampleList>

<Callout title="Zeit vor Ort (When before where)">
Usually, the time specification (Zeit) comes before the place specification (Ort).<br/>
This is different from English, where the place usually comes first.
</Callout>

## The verb stays in second position

Time or place can also go at the start of a sentence, which puts the focus on it. English does this too. If, for example, you listed what you do every day, you would say "On Monday I work, on Tuesday I study...", starting each sentence with the time, rather than the subject.

German does the same, but the verb **always** stays the second element of the sentence. That means the subject moves after the verb to create a "time-verb-subject" structure.

<ExampleList>
  <Example de="Morgen **lerne** ich Deutsch." en="Tomorrow I am **learning** German." />
  <Example de="Im Urlaub **schlafe** ich jeden Tag." en="On vacation I **sleep** every day." />
  <Example de="Zu Hause **lernen** wir Deutsch." en="At home we are **learning** German." />
</ExampleList>

<Callout type="warning" title="Common mistake">
"Heute ich arbeite" is wrong. The verb must be second: "Heute **arbeite** ich".
</Callout>

Even when the time or place is long, it still counts as one element, so the verb comes right after it.

<ExampleList>
  <Example
    de="timeplace(Montags vor der Arbeit) verb(koche) noun(ich)."
    en="timeplace(On Mondays before work) noun(I) verb(cook)."
  />
  <Example
    de="timeplace(Im Garten vor dem Haus) verb(steht) noun(ein Baum)."
    en="timeplace(In the garden in front of the house) verb(stands) noun(a tree)."
  />
</ExampleList>
