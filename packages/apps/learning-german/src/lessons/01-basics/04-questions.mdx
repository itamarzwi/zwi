## Types of questions

German has two types of questions:
1. Yes/no questions
2. Question-word questions (or "W-questions").

Yes/no questions are simple questions that can be answered with a single "yes" or "no":


<ExampleList>
  <Example en="Are you learning German?" />
  <Example en="Does Anna live in Marburg?" />
  <Example en="Is the book new?" />
</ExampleList>


"W-questions", like who, where or when, ask questions that always require full sentence answers:

<ExampleList>
  <Example en="Where do you live?" />
  <Example en="Who is Anna?" />
  <Example en="Why are you learning German?" />
</ExampleList>

In German, W-questions have their own sentence structure, while yes/no questions follow a modified version of the basic sentence structure

## Yes/no questions

To turn a statement into a yes/no question, all you have to do is move the verb over to the first position:

<ExampleList>
  <Example de="Du verb(lernst) Deutsch." en="You are verb(learning) German." />
  <Example de="verb(Lernst) du Deutsch?" en="Are you verb(learning) German?" />
</ExampleList>

<ExampleList>
  <Example de="Anna verb(wohnt) in Marburg." en="Anna verb(lives) in Marburg" />
  <Example de="verb(Wohnt) Anna in Marburg?" en="verb(Does) Anna verb(live) in Marburg?" />
</ExampleList>

<ExampleList>
  <Example de="Das Buch verb(ist) neu." en="The book verb(is) new" />
  <Example de="verb(Ist) das Buch neu?" en="verb(Is) the book new?" />
</ExampleList>

While the German version always looks the same, English has many different forms for yes/no questions. The above examples demonstrate this.


German has no word like "do" or "does", the verb simply jumps to the first position.

## W-question words

A W-question starts with a W-question word. Here are some common ones:

| German | English |
| ------ | ------- |
| wer? | who? |
| was? | what? |
| wo? | where? |
| woher? | where from? |
| wohin? | where to? |
| wann? | when? |
| wie? | how? |
| warum? | why? |
| wie viel? | how much? |
| wie viele? | how many? |
| welche? | which? |

In a W-question, the question word comes first and the verb stays in second position.

<ExampleList>
  <Example de="Wo verb(bist) du?" en="Where verb(are) you?" />
  <Example de="Wie verb(heißt) du?" en="What verb(is) your name?" />
  <Example de="Wann verb(essen) wir?" en="When verb(are) we verb(eating)?" />
</ExampleList>

<Callout>
  "Wie heißt du?" literally translates to "How are you called?", which is why German uses "Wie" while the English translation uses "What".
</Callout>

## Simple question words

These question words always appear the same and can mostly be used as 1-to-1 translations of their English counterparts:

| German | English |
| ------ | ------- |
| was? | what? |
| wo? | where? |
| woher? | where from? |
| wohin? | where to? |
| wann? | when? |
| wie? | how? |
| warum? | why? |

### Was, wann, wie, warum

These words translate directly to their counterparts in English and are used in the same way.

<ExampleList>
  <Example de="adverb(Was) liest das Kind?" en="adverb(What) is the child reading?" />
  <Example de="adverb(Wann) lernst du Deutsch?" en="adverb(When) do you learn German?" />
  <Example de="adverb(Wie) geht es dir?" en="adverb(How) are you?" />
  <Example de="adverb(Warum) lernst du Deutsch?" en="adverb(Why) are you learning German?" />
</ExampleList>

<Callout title="Wie geht es dir?">
"Wie geht es dir?" is the most common way of asking "How are you?". It literally translates to "How goes it to you?", and is usually shortened to "Wie geht's dir?" or even just "Wie geht's?" in everyday speech.

"dir" is the Dativ form of "du", which we haven't covered yet. For now you can just learn the phrase as a whole.
</Callout>

### Wo, woher, wohin

These three words all ask about a place, but they ask different things. **Wo** asks where something is, **woher** asks where it comes from, and **wohin** asks where it is going.

"woher" and "wohin" do not translate 1-to-1 with how you would use them in English. English separates these words and puts the second part at the end: "where ... from" and "where ... to". German keeps the word whole.

<ExampleList>
  <Example de="adverb(Wo) wohnst du?" en="adverb(Where) do you live?" />
  <Example de="adverb(Woher) kommst du?" en="adverb(Where) are you from?" />
  <Example de="adverb(Wohin) gehst du?" en="adverb(Where) are you going?" />
</ExampleList>


## Wer

| German | English |
| ------ | ------- |
| wer? | who? |
| wen? | whom? |

Wer is the first question word we encounter that changes depending on its case. If we ask about the *subject*, the question comes in the *Nominativ* case and we use **wer**. If we ask about the *direct object*, the question comes in the *Akkusativ* case and we use **wen**.

<GridTable>

| Case | who |
| ---- | --- |
| Nom. | wer |
| Akk. | wen |

</GridTable>

<ExampleList>
  <Example de="green(Wer) sieht den Hund?" en="green(Who) sees the dog?" />
  <Example de="blue(Wen) sieht der Mann?" en="blue(Whom) does the man see?" />
  <Example de="green(Wer) hat eine Katze?" en="green(Who) has a cat?" />
  <Example de="blue(Wen) hast du?" en="blue(Whom) do you have?" />
</ExampleList>

There are of course Dativ and Genitiv forms for "wer" as well (With *whom* are you walking? *Whose* cat is this?), but we do not learn them here yet.

## Which

| German | English |
| ------ | ------- |
| welche? | which? |

Welche is the only W-question word that changes with both the case and the gender. It always accompanies a noun and declines exactly like der, die and das; the Akkusativ only changes in masculine form.

<GenderedTable
  label="Case"
  rows={[
    ["Nom.", "welcher", "welches", "welche", "welche"],
    ["Akk.", "welchen", "welches", "welche", "welche"],
  ]}
/>

In the Nominativ, the noun after welcher is the subject:

<ExampleList>
  <Example de="green(Welcher Hund) schläft?" en="green(Which dog) is sleeping?" />
  <Example de="green(Welches Buch) ist neu?" en="green(Which book) is new?" />
  <Example de="green(Welche Katze) ist klein?" en="green(Which cat) is small?" />
  <Example de="green(Welche Hunde) schlafen?" en="green(Which dogs) are sleeping?" />
</ExampleList>

In the Akkusativ, it is the direct object. Only the masculine changes:

<ExampleList>
  <Example de="blue(Welchen Hund) siehst du?" en="blue(Which dog) do you see?" />
  <Example de="blue(Welches Buch) liest du?" en="blue(Which book) are you reading?" />
  <Example de="blue(Welche Katze) hast du?" en="blue(Which cat) do you have?" />
  <Example de="blue(Welche Hunde) siehst du?" en="blue(Which dogs) do you see?" />
</ExampleList>

Both cases can also appear in the same question:

<ExampleList>
  <Example de="green(Welcher Mann) hat blue(welchen Hund)?" en="green(Which man) has blue(which dog)?" />
  <Example de="green(Welche Frau) liest blue(welches Buch)?" en="green(Which woman) is reading blue(which book)?" />
</ExampleList>

## Wie viel und wie viele?

| German | English |
| ------ | ------- |
| wie viel? | how much? |
| wie viele? | how many? |

These questions translate 1-to-1 to the English "How much" and "How many" (viel=much, viele=many). We use "Wie viel" for uncountables like money or time, and "wie viele" for countable things with a plural noun.

<ExampleList>
  <Example de="**Wie viel** Zeit hast du?" en="**How much** time do you have?" />
  <Example de="**Wie viele** Bücher hast du?" en="**How many** books do you have?" />
</ExampleList>
