## What are cases?

Every noun in a sentence has a case. A case tells you what role the noun plays in the sentence: Is this noun acting? Is it being acted upon? Is it stationary, or moving? We haven't talked about cases until now, but you have already been using one all along: the Nominativ case.

### Nominativ: the default

Nominativ is the "default" case, the one we use when referring to nouns on their own. The subject of a sentence always takes the Nominativ case. The subject is the one doing the action, or the thing the sentence is about. To find it, ask "who or what?", for example:

"Der Mann schläft" (the man is sleeping).  
Who is sleeping? Der Mann.

When a noun comes after sein, as in "Das ist ein Hund", the noun takes the Nominativ case as well.  
The articles you've learned so far (der, die, das, ein, eine) are all in the Nominativ form.

### The four cases

German has four cases:

1. Nominativ: the subject, the one doing the action.
2. Akkusativ: the direct object, the one the action happens to.
3. Dativ: the indirect object, the one who receives something, or "supporting".
4. Genitiv: possession, whose something is.

For now, we only care about the first two - Nominativ and Akkusativ. They are the most common ones, and you can already form many sentences with them. Dativ and Genitiv will come later.

### Nominativ and Akkusativ in sentences

In a simple sentence, the Nominativ is the one doing the action, and the Akkusativ is the one the action is being performed on. To find the Akkusativ, ask "whom or what?". It is almost always the Object of the sentence, which comes after the verb.  

"Der Mann sieht den Hund".  
whom does the man see? Den Hund.

## What changes?

Now that you know what cases are, let's see what actually changes between the Nominativ and the Akkusativ case.  
The good news: only masculine nouns change. Feminine nouns, neuter nouns and plurals look exactly the same in both cases.

### Articles

In the masculine, der becomes de**n** and ein becomes ein**en** in the Akkusativ. Feminine and neuter keep die, eine, das and ein, and the plural keeps die.

The same goes for possessives, because they work like ein: mein becomes mein**en**, dein becomes dein**en**, and so on. Again, this only applies to masculine nouns.

### Adjectives

The masculine adjective endings change too. In the Akkusativ it is always -en: den gut**en** Hund, einen gut**en** Hund. In the Nominativ it was -e after der and -er after ein. Feminine, neuter and plural adjectives keep the endings they have in the Nominativ.

<Callout title="Easy to remember">
Feminine, neuter and plural never change. Whenever a masculine noun is the object, look for the extra n.
</Callout>
