## What is the role of the Dativ case?

You already know two cases. The Nominativ is the subject of the sentence, and the Akkusativ is the direct object of the sentence. But there are nouns that fulfill a third role in a sentence: someone¹ who receives something, someone that is accompanying the subject, or someone that the action is being done for. That is the Dativ, the indirect object.

Compare these two sentences in English:

<ExampleList>
  <Example en="green(I) bring blue(the child) to orange(the mother)." />
  <Example en="green(I) bring blue(the mother) to orange(the child)." />
</ExampleList>

Both sentences use the same words, but they mean different things. The position of the nouns in comparison to the word "to" tells you which noun is fulfilling which role. The blue word is the direct object (what you are bringing), and the orange word is the indirect object (whom you are bringing it to).

So in English, the word "to"², along with word order, is used to distinguish the roles of the nouns. In German, the case tells you.

What do I bring? The thing being brought is the Akkusativ.  
To whom am I bringing it? The one receiving it is the Dativ.  

<Footnote>1. We say "someone", but it is not necessarily a person. Any noun can fulfill any role.</Footnote>

<Footnote>2. English uses "to" to indicate the indirect object in this example, but that is not the only Dativ marker in English. Sometimes it is "to", sometimes "for", sometimes there is no extra word at all ("I throw you the ball"), and with some verbs it translates completely differently.</Footnote>

## The Dativ articles

Unlike the Akkusativ, the Dativ changes the declensions for every gender:

  label="Case"
  rows={[

    ["Akk.", "de**n** gut*en*\nein**en** gut*en*\ngut**en**", "da**s** gut*e*\nein gut**es**\ngut**es**", "di**e** gut*e*\nein**e** gut*e*\ngut**e**", "di**e** gut*en*\nmein**e** gut*en*\ngut**e**"],
    ["Dat.", "de**m** gut*en*\nein**em** gut*en*\ngut**em**", "de**m** gut*en*\nein**em** gut*en*\ngut**em**", "de**r** gut*en*\nein**er** gut*en*\ngut**er**", "de**n** gut*en*\nmein**en** gut*en*\ngut**en**"],
  ]}
/>

Masculine and neuter share the same forms: dem and einem. Feminine becomes der and einer. The plural becomes den. And possessives follow ein as always: meinem, meinem, meiner, meinen.

The Dativ adjective is easy 😄. After an article, it always ends in -en. With no article, the adjective takes the ending that the definite article would have had, same as Nominativ and Akkusativ: mit kalt**em** Wasser (with cold water), mit frisch**er** Milch (with fresh milk).

### The plural -n

Plural nouns that do not already end in -n or -s get an extra -n in the Dativ case.

<ExampleList>
  <Example de="die Kinder → orange(den Kindern)" en="the children" />
  <Example de="die Hunde → orange(den Hunden)" en="the dogs" />
  <Example de="die Frauen → orange(den Frauen)" en="the women (already ends in -n)" />
  <Example de="die Autos → orange(den Autos)" en="the cars (ends in -s)" />
</ExampleList>


In these following examples, green is the Nominativ, blue is the Akkusativ and orange is the Dativ.

<ExampleList>
  <Example de="green(Anna) gibt orange(der Frau) blue(ein Buch)." en="green(Anna) gives orange(the woman) blue(a book)." note="Das Buch: neuter" />
  <Example de="green(Wir) kaufen orange(dem Kind) blue(einen Ball)." en="green(We) buy orange(the child) blue(a ball)." note="Der Ball: masculine" />
  <Example de="green(Ich) gebe orange(dem Mann) blue(den Schlüssel)." en="green(I) give blue(the key) to orange(the man)." note="Der Schlüssel: masculine" />
  <Example de="green(Ich) schreibe orange(meiner Mutter) blue(einen Brief)." en="green(I) write blue(a letter) to orange(my mother)." note="Der Brief: masculine" />
</ExampleList>

## Word order

Because the case already marks who gets what, we can change the word order without changing the meaning:

<ExampleList>
  <Example de="green(Ich) bringe orange(dem Vater) blue(das Kind)." en="green(I) bring blue(the child) to orange(the father)." />
  <Example de="green(Ich) bringe blue(das Kind) orange(dem Vater)." en="green(I) bring blue(the child) to orange(the father)." />
</ExampleList>

Dem Vater is in the Dativ, so the father is the one **to whom** the child is being brought, no matter where it appears in the sentence. This is different from English! We have been using "to", but we could technically also translate the sentences in the following way:

<ExampleList>
  <Example de="green(Ich) bringe orange(dem Kind) blue(den Vater)." en="green(I) bring orange(the child) blue(the father)." />
  <Example de="green(Ich) bringe orange(dem Vater) blue(das Kind)." en="green(I) bring orange(the father) blue(the child)." />
  <Example de="green(Ich) bringe blue(das Kind) orange(dem Vater)." en="green(I) bring orange(the father) blue(the child)." />
</ExampleList>

Notice how in the last two examples, the English translation is exactly the same, despite the word order being different in German!

### Dativ first

*Technically*, both word orders above are correct, but they are not equals. When a sentence has a Dativ noun and an Akkusativ noun, the Dativ normally comes first:

<ExampleList>
  <Example de="green(Ich) gebe orange(dem Mann) blue(den Schlüssel)." en="green(I) give blue(the key) to orange(the man)." note="✓ The usual order" />
  <Example de="green(Ich) schreibe orange(meiner Mutter) blue(einen Brief)." en="green(I) write blue(a letter) to orange(my mother)." note="✓ The usual order" />
  <Example de="green(Ich) bringe blue(das Kind) orange(dem Vater)." en="green(I) bring blue(the child) to orange(the father)." note="Correct, but sounds unusual." />
</ExampleList>

With a feminine Dativ noun, the other order can be really confusing:

<ExampleList>
  <Example de="green(Ich) bringe orange(der Mutter) blue(das Kind)." en="green(I) bring blue(the child) to orange(the mother)." note="✓ Clear" />
  <Example de="green(Ich) bringe blue(das Kind) orange(der Mutter)." en="green(I) bring blue(the child) to orange(the mother)." note="✗ This reads as: “I bring the mother's child”" />
</ExampleList>

"Das Kind der Mutter" is also how we would say "the mother's child" in German. This uses the fourth case, the Genitiv, which we haven't learned yet, but this is a good reason to always stick to Dativ first.

When pronouns get added, the rules change. We will cover them in the "Dativ pronouns" lesson.

## Where or where to?

JESS - Cont. here

The Dativ has a second big job, and it shows up with prepositions. Compare these two sentences:

<ExampleList>
  <Example de="Ich gehe blue(ins Kino)." en="I am going to the cinema." />
  <Example de="Ich bin orange(im Kino)." en="I am at the cinema." />
</ExampleList>

Ins is short for in das (Akkusativ), and im is short for in dem (Dativ). Same preposition, same noun, different case.

Here is an even clearer pair. The verb and the preposition are the same, and only the case changes the meaning:

<ExampleList>
  <Example de="Ich laufe blue(in den Park)." en="I run into the park." />
  <Example de="Ich laufe orange(im Park)." en="I run in the park." />
</ExampleList>

In the first sentence I am outside the park and run into it. In the second sentence I am already in the park, running around inside it.

### Wohin? and Wo?

You know these question words already. They are the key to choosing the case:

1. Wohin? (where to?) asks for a destination. The answer takes the Akkusativ.
2. Wo? (where?) asks for a location. The answer takes the Dativ.

<ExampleList>
  <Example de="Wohin fahrt ihr? Wir fahren blue(in die Stadt)." en="Where are you driving to? We are driving into town." />
  <Example de="Wo wohnt ihr? Wir wohnen orange(in der Stadt)." en="Where do you live? We live in the city." />
  <Example de="Wohin springt die Katze? Sie springt blue(auf das Sofa)." en="Where is the cat jumping? It jumps onto the sofa." />
  <Example de="Wo schläft die Katze? Sie schläft orange(auf dem Sofa)." en="Where is the cat sleeping? It sleeps on the sofa." />
</ExampleList>

<Callout title="Movement alone is not enough">
  Running in the park is movement, but it stays inside the park, so it is Dativ. The question is not "is something moving?", it is "is something going to a new place?". If yes, Akkusativ. If it stays where it is, Dativ.
</Callout>

## Legen and liegen

German often has two verbs for this: one for the action of putting something somewhere, and one for the state of being there.

1. legen: to put something down, to lay. Something goes to a new place, so Akkusativ.
2. liegen: to lie, to be lying somewhere. Nothing moves, so Dativ.

<ExampleList>
  <Example de="Ich verb(lege) das Buch auf blue(den Tisch)." en="I put the book on the table." />
  <Example de="Das Buch verb(liegt) auf orange(dem Tisch)." en="The book is lying on the table." />
  <Example de="Anna verb(legt) das Handy auf blue(das Bett)." en="Anna puts the phone on the bed." />
  <Example de="Das Handy verb(liegt) auf orange(dem Bett)." en="The phone is lying on the bed." />
  <Example de="Er verb(legt) die Schlüssel in blue(die Tasche)." en="He puts the keys in the bag." />
  <Example de="Die Schlüssel verb(liegen) in orange(der Tasche)." en="The keys are in the bag." />
</ExampleList>

Legen is regular. Liegen is a different verb, and it is the one you use whenever something is lying somewhere.

<Callout title="Not every preposition does this">
  Only nine prepositions switch between the Akkusativ and the Dativ like this, among them in and auf. They have their own lesson later in this module. Other prepositions always take the same case, no matter what.
</Callout>
